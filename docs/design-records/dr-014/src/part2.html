Chapter Five

The orb: one control, two jobs

Docked, it is a home button. Tapped, it becomes the centre the navigation radiates from. Those are not two components that swap places; they are one element with two declared transforms and a 500ms transition between them, and that identity is the whole trick.

Most menus are separate from the thing that opens them. A hamburger is a button; the drawer is a panel; the button stays put while the panel arrives from somewhere else. The relationship between them is learned, not seen — you press the icon, something appears elsewhere, and you infer the connection.

The hub refuses that separation. When you tap the orb, the orb itself travels to the middle of the screen, and the menu grows out of where it lands. There is never a moment when the thing you touched and the thing that opened are two objects. The dial is literally an annulus around the orb; the hole in its middle is the orb's parking space, sized from the orb's own tokens.

Read that translateY carefully, because it is doing something slightly clever. The orb is anchored to the bottom of the viewport, so to place its centre at the viewport's centre it needs to move up by (distance from bottom edge to its own centre) minus (half the viewport). --orb-bottom is the gap below it; 50% in a transform resolves against the element's own size, so it is half the orb; and 50dvh is half the dynamic viewport height. The result is dead centre, with no measurement, no JavaScript, and — critically — dvh rather than vh, so it stays centred as a mobile browser's toolbars collapse and re-expand.

Why the centring rule is scoped to open and not closing

There is no explicit "return home" transform. The centring rule applies only at [data-phase='open']; the moment the phase flips to closing, that rule stops matching and the base docked transform takes over. The browser transitions from wherever the orb currently is back to the dock. One declaration, both directions — the reversibility constraint from Chapter 4, paying off in three lines of CSS.

5.2 Why the trigger is not also a link

An obvious objection: if the orb is the home button, tapping it should go home. It does not — it opens the dial, and Home is the first wedge inside.

This was a deliberate trade and it is worth defending, because it is the one place the design knowingly costs a user something. Going home became two taps instead of one.

What it bought: a single, unambiguous interaction contract. A control that navigates on tap and opens a menu on long-press is a control with a hidden mode, and hidden modes on touch surfaces are how people end up somewhere they did not ask to be. The orb has exactly one behaviour, its aria-haspopup="menu" and aria-expanded describe that behaviour accurately, and nothing about it is discovered by holding it down longer than usual.

It also bought the geometry. If tapping the orb navigated, the dial would need a different trigger, and the "menu grows out of the thing you touched" identity — the reason the shape means anything — would be gone.

The mitigation is that Home is the first wedge, at twelve o'clock on the inner deck: the largest, closest, most predictable target on the dial. Two taps, both of them in the same place, neither requiring the user to read anything.

5.3 The wrapper that generates no box

The component's root element is one of the more unusual lines in the module:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|715-719|A wrapper that is only a state carrier}}

display: contents removes the wrapper from the box tree entirely — its children are laid out as though they were children of its parent. That is precisely what is wanted here, because every one of those children is position: fixed and positions itself against the viewport. A real wrapper box would add nothing except a risk: any wrapper that later acquired a filter, a transform or a backdrop-filter would become a containing block for its fixed descendants and quietly break all of them.

What the wrapper is for is the data-phase attribute. Every animated layer in the hub is selected as a descendant of .radial-hub[data-phase='…'], so one attribute change on one element re-declares the resting state of the orb, the aura, the scrim, the overlay, the blur, the dial, all sixteen wedge wrappers, the hairlines and the foot pill simultaneously. That is a CSS state machine with a single write, and it is why the whole thing animates in lockstep without an orchestrator.

5.4 What sits around the dial

The opened hub is not only the dial. Three other pieces share the overlay, and each exists for a specific reason.

The scrim.
A transparent, full-screen <button> beneath the blur that closes the menu when pressed. It is a button rather than a div with a click handler so it is focusable, nameable and keyboard-operable by default — it carries a translated "Close" label. Because it sits below the blur and the dial in the stack, only genuinely empty regions dismiss.
The foot pill.
A floating capsule at the bottom of the overlay holding identity, settings, sign-out and an explicit close button — signed out, it holds a single "Sign in" call to action instead. These are the destinations that are about you rather than about the site, and putting them on the dial as wedges would have implied they are peers of Library and Arcade. It is the one L4 glass surface in the hub: it frosts what passes under it rather than hiding it.
The aura.
Purely decorative: a disc the size of the orb plus three orbiting blobs, all in the accent colour, all under the goo filter, so they fuse into one wobbling liquid mass that bulges and necks as the blobs swing. It is a separate fixed layer rather than a pseudo-element on the orb — Chapter 18 explains why putting it on the orb would have destroyed the mark.

The explicit close button in the foot deserves a note. There are already three ways to close the dial: tap the orb again, tap the scrim, press Escape. A fourth, visible, labelled control is redundant for anyone who knows the first three — and it is the only one that is discoverable to someone who does not. It is also the only close affordance that survives on a touch device with no keyboard and no expectation that background taps dismiss. It is filled in the accent colour, which makes it the single most prominent control in the overlay after the dial itself.

5.5 The desktop rail is not a second implementation

From 1120px the shell also shows a persistent left rail, and it would be easy to read that as an admission that the dial does not work on desktop. It is not. The rail renders the same SIDEBAR_NAV, applies the same auth and admin gating, and computes active state the same way; it adds only live badge counts, which a dial sector has no room for. The hub remains mounted and functional at every width.

The reasoning is that a wide screen has spare horizontal space and a mouse, and under those conditions a persistent list is strictly faster than any menu that has to open — no state transition, no motion, no dismissal. The dial's advantage is that it costs no layout space, and layout space is only scarce on small screens. Serving both is not inconsistency; it is the same catalog rendered at the density each viewport can afford.

Chapter Six

The material

The dial is not drawn on a coloured disc. It is a hole cut in a frosted plate — an expanding circular blur with translucent sectors over it. That choice is what makes a sixteen-sector menu covering most of the screen feel like something that opened rather than something that landed.

The platform's design language is Radial Avant-Garde Glass: Apple's Liquid Glass material, used theatrically rather than literally, draped over a radial information architecture. The material half of that name is a large system in its own right — an elevation ladder of five explicit CSS classes, a specular rim glint that tracks a global scene light, a pointer-tracked diffuse highlight, lens-model edge refraction, an optional chromatic prism, travelling liquid sheens, and a drifting aurora canvas painted on the document body that every backdrop-filter on the page samples.

The hub uses almost none of it. That restraint is deliberate and worth explaining.

6.1 What the hub takes from the material system

The pattern: the hub takes the material's depth and its liquidity, and declines every optic whose job is to make a surface look lit. A menu is a temporary thing the user summoned; it should read as an atmospheric change, not as a new physical object with its own lighting.

6.2 The blur is the menu

The single most consequential material decision: there is no drawn disc behind the dial. The layer that fills the screen when the menu opens is a backdrop-filter with a clip-path circle animating from radius zero to 150vmax, and only a whisper of background tint — 20% of the page background mixed with transparency — so it reads as blur rather than as a coloured circle.

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|972-988|The expanding circular blur}}

Three things follow from this that would not follow from a drawn disc.

The page stays visible. You can see, blurred, where you were. The menu is an overlay in the literal sense — a change in the optical properties of the page, not a replacement for it. Dismiss it and the frost contracts back to a point; nothing was ever hidden.

The expansion is free. Animating a clip-path circle's radius is a compositor-friendly operation on one element. A drawn disc growing to cover the viewport would be a large paint region changing size every frame.

It cannot be the wrong colour. A drawn disc needs a fill, a fill needs a token, and a token that works on a white page and a near-black page and a yellow-accented high-contrast page is a genuinely hard thing to choose. Blur has no colour of its own. It samples whatever is behind it, in whatever theme, and is correct by construction.

The menu's surface is not a colour that had to be chosen. It is the page you were already looking at, out of focus.

6.3 Where translucency had to be walked back

Translucency is correct for the blur and wrong for the sectors, and discovering that cost a shipped defect — commit c631cb4, "the radial menu was invisible on a light page."

The sectors were originally filled with color-mix(in srgb, var(--site-surface) 60%, transparent): 60% of the surface token, 40% transparent. Reasonable-looking code. The trap is that --site-surface is itself translucent in this material — the design language is explicit that surfaces are tints over the aurora, not paper. So the expression was a tint of a tint, and over a light page it resolved to very nearly nothing. The sectors disappeared; the dial was a rim and some floating glyphs.

The repair uses the opaque companion token the material ships for exactly this case, and then does one more thing that is the actually interesting part:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|1164-1169|The sector fill}}

That 6% of --site-text is what separates the dial from the page. On the default theme both the page background and the opaque surface token are white; without the mix, the sectors would be white on white and only the rim would show. Mixing toward the ink adapts automatically in a way a fixed colour cannot: a touch darker than a light page, a touch lighter than a dark one, in any theme anyone ever authors, including ones that do not exist yet.

The generalisable rule

When a surface must be distinguishable from an unknown background, do not pick a colour — pick a direction. color-mix(surface, text, 6%) says "slightly toward the ink", and every theme's ink is by definition contrasting with every theme's background. A hex value says "this specific grey", which is a bet on a palette.

6.4 The plate under the sectors

Beneath the sectors is the dial's own background — the "divider bed". Its job is mundane: the sectors tile the disc with thin angular gaps between them, and the bed is what shows through those gaps as the hairline dividers. It also stops the disc dissolving into a light page.

Its colour is a shade darker than the sectors that sit on it, built the same way: color-mix(in srgb, var(--site-surface-opaque) 86%, var(--site-text) 14%). The value 14% versus the sectors' 6% is the entire visual language of the dividers — they are not drawn lines, they are the bed showing through.

This bed is also the villain of Chapter 16, where a perfectly sensible earlier choice for its colour made the whole dial flash black on open the moment the decks started spinning. It is a good example of a value that is correct until a motion change makes it visible.

6.5 Degradation, and where it is handled

The platform's rule is that accessibility and performance degradation happen in the token layer and the shared glass classes, not in components — a component that branches on prefers-reduced-transparency by hand is considered a bug. The hub obeys this, with the consequence that the interesting behaviour is in media queries rather than in TypeScript.

What the hub looks like under each degradation

Note what is not in that table: the interaction. In every degraded mode the dial still opens, still has sixteen labelled targets, still traps focus sensibly, still closes on Escape. The material is the thing that degrades. The navigation is not optional.

Chapter Seven

The mark that became the button

The RMH mark is a six-fold radial spirograph: several hundred overlapping curves in one continuous path. Putting it inside a 44-pixel disc — and keeping it legible there — required deciding what a logo is for when it is also a control.

The mark predates the redesign. It is an Inkscape-generated spirograph with its path baked, six-fold rotationally symmetric, drawn as line art. At poster size it is genuinely beautiful; at 20 pixels in a header it is a grey smudge, which is roughly what it had been reduced to.

The radial premise offered a much better job for it. A mark whose own construction is radial, placed at the centre a radial interface orbits, is not decoration — it is a statement of what the geometry means. So the mark went into the orb, at 74% of the orb's size, and the work became: make a few hundred overlapping curves survive being rendered at 44 pixels.

7.1 Hairline weight is not a style choice

The controlling problem is stroke weight. The path is dense: at any reasonable weight, overlapping curves bleed into each other and the whole thing fills in as a solid muddy blob. The component's own comment is direct about it:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|895-903|Why the stroke is 0.8}}

The mark is authored in a 150-unit viewBox and rendered into roughly 44 CSS pixels, so it is scaled down by about 3.4×. A stroke width of 0.8 in that space is a genuinely sub-pixel filigree on screen — which is the point. The result reads as a delicate white tracery rather than a heavy disc, and the six-fold symmetry survives even when individual curves do not fully resolve. What the eye recovers at that size is the rotational structure, and rotational structure is exactly what the interface is about.

An implementation detail worth stealing

The path carries pathLength={1}. That normalises its length to one unit, so stroke-dasharray: 1; stroke-dashoffset: 1 is a complete, unfilled outline regardless of the path's real length — which for this path is enormous and would otherwise have to be measured. The bloom animation then just runs stroke-dashoffset from 1 to 0 and the mark draws itself, resolution- and geometry-independent. Change the artwork and the animation still works.

On mount, the mark scales and rotates out of the orb's core while its stroke draws itself:

Two animations, deliberately different lengths — the geometry settles at 1.05s while the stroke finishes drawing at 1.3s, so the mark arrives and then completes rather than doing both at once. Both are wrapped in prefers-reduced-motion: no-preference, so a user who has asked for less motion simply gets the finished mark.

Worth noting what this animation is not: it is not tied to opening the menu. It runs once, when the shell mounts. It is the site introducing itself, not a response to an interaction — which is why it is a keyframe animation rather than a transition, the one place in the hub where a one-shot is correct, because there is no reverse state to return to.

7.3 The ink problem, and the first version of a mistake

The mark sits on the orb's solid accent fill, so the correct ink for it is that surface's paired foreground: --site-accent-fg. Every theme authors that pairing, every accent preset overrides it, and a runtime contrast check — ensureReadableAccent() — refuses to ship a pair that fails. Naming the token makes the mark correct in every theme, including user-authored ones.

That is the current code. An earlier revision instead used mix-blend-mode: difference, reasoning that white blended in difference mode against any backdrop yields that backdrop's exact inverse, and therefore maximum contrast automatically, for free, in every theme.

It is a genuinely elegant idea and it is wrong, for a reason that has nothing to do with colour theory: a blend mode only composites against the backdrop inside the element's own stacking context. Put the blended element in a stacking context that does not contain the thing you meant to blend against, and it inverts the wrong backdrop — or nothing at all. That is exactly how the dial's glyphs ended up white on white, which is Chapter 20, and it is why the comment explaining the current token-based approach in radial.css ends with a warning about the revision it replaced.

"Automatic" contrast that depends on stacking context is not automatic. It is a bet on a layer tree that the compositor is entitled to change.

7.4 The mark as a loading state

One more consequence of promoting the mark: it gave the platform a house loading indicator. ui/radial-loader.tsx is the same visual idea — blobs orbiting a pulsing core, fused by the same goo filter — and it is what the shared Spinner primitive renders. So a wait anywhere on the site speaks the same language as the hub.

This is a small thing that indicates the redesign was working. When a navigation control's visual vocabulary is coherent enough that unrelated parts of the product can borrow it and look native, the vocabulary is real. It is also, per the audit, not free: finding AUD-302 records that on phones the feed swapped a full-viewport goo loader for the whole page and produced a measured layout shift of 0.13–0.21, well past the 0.1 threshold. A house style that is applied without a size reservation is still a regression.
