27.1 The shape of the five days

Read as a curve, the history has three distinct phases, and the proportions are worth noting because they are typical and rarely admitted.

Day one: eight commits, the whole design. Shell, feed, hub, wedges, mark, glass, spacing, safe areas. Everything that this document's Parts I through III describe as the concept and the geometry existed by the end of 24 July.

Days three and four: seven commits, five of them fixes. The material landed, and with it the entire class of contrast failures. Four of the five fixes are the same bug wearing different clothes.

Day five: two commits, both audit remediation. Neither adds a feature. Both are lists of specific, measured, located defects with fix directions attached.

The design took a day. Making it correct in three themes, at thirteen viewports, with a home indicator and a cookie banner and a screen reader, took four more — and was not finished. That ratio is the honest summary of what building a control like this costs.

27.2 The commit that removed a state

One entry deserves attention because it is a deletion rather than an addition. bfd84b6, "hub opens/closes as one synchronous motion", removed a phase from the state machine.

The original design opened in two beats: first the orb glided to the centre (centering), then the menu bloomed around it (open). That is the intuitive reading of the effect — cause, then consequence — and it is what the module's README still describes in one paragraph that was not updated.

It was worse. Two beats meant the orb arrived and then paused, and the pause read as latency rather than as choreography. It also doubled the state machine's complexity for the reversal case: interrupting during centering and interrupting during open are different situations, and both had to unwind correctly.

Collapsing them means the blur starts expanding while the orb is still travelling, which on paper is wrong — the menu begins before its origin has arrived — and in practice is much better, because both motions share the same front-loaded curve and therefore complete their visible portion together. The Phase type's comment still records the change: "closed → open (orb glides to centre AS the menu blooms) → closing → closed."

A note on documentation drift

The README's description of the hub — "tapping the fixed RMH orb glides it to the centre of the screen, then opens the menu" — describes the two-beat version that was removed. It is a small, harmless inaccuracy, and it is a good illustration of why this record leans on the code and the commit messages rather than on prose descriptions: the component's own type comment was updated in the commit that changed the behaviour, and the README was not.

Chapter Twenty-eight

Six patterns

Every defect in this project is an instance of one of six patterns. None of them is specific to radial menus, and all six are worth carrying to the next component.

28.1 Derived contrast that depends on the layer tree

Instances: AUD-504 precursor (dial glyphs), the logo mark, the pointer drop's plate. Commits: 28e03b2, 454a762, 6e6c184.

Blend modes composite against a stacking context, not against what you see. Any "automatic contrast" built on mix-blend-mode is a bet on a layer tree the compositor is entitled to change — and in the pointer drop's case, a bet that does not fail under software rasterisation, so testing cannot catch it.

The rule: name the paired foreground token, and put the ink inside the element that carries the background.

28.2 A tint of a tint

Instance: the whole dial invisible on a light page. Commit: c631cb4.

In a material where surface tokens are already translucent, color-mix(surface 60%, transparent) is 60% of something that was already partly transparent. The expression looks like it produces a visible surface and produces almost nothing.

The rule: when a value must be visible against an unknown background, build it on the opaque companion token and mix toward the ink — a direction, not a colour.

28.3 A value whose correctness depends on being covered

Instance: the divider bed flashing black once the decks spun. Commit: d308f26.

The bed was pure black, which was correct while the sectors covered it at every frame. Adding rotation and a sub-1 resting scale uncovered a 23-pixel ring of it for 550 milliseconds.

The rule: if a layer is only acceptable because something covers it, either write that assumption down at the declaration or make the layer safe to see. Adding motion to a layer changes the requirements of every layer beneath it.

28.4 Chrome that does not publish its footprint

Instances: AUD-003 and its eight dependents — the cookie banner, feed cards, the pricing scroll cue, the help chips, the settings rows, the shop tab strip, landscape viewport height. Commits: 8a20189, 4419355.

A fixed element is a claim on screen space. If that claim is not published in a form layouts can consume, every layout will eventually collide with it — and the collisions will be found one page at a time, by users.

The rule: publish the footprint as tokens (--site-hub-orb-size, --site-hub-orb-inset), derive clearance from those tokens rather than guessing (--site-floating-reserve), coordinate co-located chrome explicitly (the floating-bottom stack), and scope that coordination to the elements involved rather than to the viewport where the problem was first noticed.

28.5 Text boxes that spill in the constrained direction

Instance: AUD-504(c) — "Adaptive Intelligence" clipped, "Predictions" overflowing the disc, then "Communities" truncated by the over-correction. Commit: feeec2c.

An axis-aligned label box inside a wedge grows angularly in width — across the clip — and radially in height, into the band, where there is room. The instinct is to widen the box; the correct move is to narrow it and let the text wrap.

The rule: identify which axis is bounded by the clip before choosing which dimension to spend. And when constraining a text box, prefer wrapping to truncation: a truncated label is a label that no longer names its destination.

28.6 Decoration in the same pixels as information

Instances: the goo filter chewing glyphs; the pointer drop swelling largest exactly over a control's label (AUD-503); the feed wheel's rake fading cards to 18% opacity (AUD-118); the full-viewport goo loader replacing the page and producing a 0.21 layout shift (AUD-302).

Four separate effects, one failure: a visual flourish placed where the reader's attention has to be. In each case the flourish was working exactly as designed.

The rule: a decorative effect gets its own layer, its own opacity floor, or its own cap. The dial split into art and hit layers; the cursor capped its diameter and thinned its fill; the wheel needed an opacity floor and a print reset; the loader needed skeletons that reserve its space. Same rule, four shapes.

Not one of these six is about radial menus. All six are about what happens when a correct value acquires a dependency nobody wrote down.

28.7 What the ledger says about process

Three observations, offered as the practical conclusion of this part.

The audit found what code review did not. Every one of these defects survived a commit that was written carefully, with comments, by someone who understood the system. What caught them was rendering forty routes at thirteen viewports in three themes and looking at the output. There is no substitute available.

The comments are the deliverable. The most valuable artefacts this project produced are not the 521 lines of component; they are the comment blocks explaining why the bed is light, why the glyphs live inside their sectors, why touch-action instead of a scroll lock, and why the pointer drop does not use the cheap CSS goo. Each one is a bug that cannot recur.

Systemic fixes beat instance fixes. 89496ed — the :where() foreground pairings in globals.css — closed a whole class of contrast bugs site-wide rather than the two instances that had been reported. AUD-003's remediation order is explicit about the same principle: fix the coordination once and eight dependent findings heal.
