Chapter Eight

From pie to annulus

A pie slice is the obvious shape for a radial menu and the wrong one. Its area is concentrated at the rim, its point converges on the exact spot the orb needs to occupy, and its centroid drifts outward as the slice narrows. Every one of the dial's geometric decisions follows from replacing the pie with a ring segment.

8.1 Four problems with the pie

The point is in the way. Sixteen pie slices converge on a single point at the centre of the disc, and that point is exactly where the orb parks. Either the orb covers the convergence — hiding the tips of sixteen targets — or the slices are truncated, at which point they are annulus sectors and one may as well say so.

Area is in the wrong place. A sector's area grows with the square of the radius, so most of a pie slice's area is in its outer third. The glyph and label, to sit in the visual middle of the slice, have to sit far out toward the rim; the inner two-thirds is a large region that belongs to the target but reads as empty.

The centroid moves as the count changes. A pie slice's centroid sits at 2r sin(θ/2) / (3·θ/2) from the centre — a function of the slice's angular width. Add a destination, all the slices narrow, and every glyph shifts radially outward for no reason the user can see. A ring segment's radial midpoint is (r₀ + r₁) / 2, which does not depend on the angle at all: glyphs stay on a fixed circle regardless of how many destinations exist.

You cannot stack pies. Two concentric decks of pie slices share their inner region, so the inner deck's slices are the inner parts of the outer deck's slices. There is nowhere to put a second level. Two rings of annulus sectors nest naturally, which is the whole reason the double-deck solution in Chapter 9 is available at all.

8.3 The dial's size

The dial's outer dimension is a single clamp, and it is doing more work than it looks:

--dial: min(86vw, 70vh, 460px);

86vw keeps a 7% margin on each side at every width, so the dial never touches the screen edge on a phone. 70vh is the constraint that actually binds in landscape — on an 844×390 device, 70vh is 273px, which is what stops the dial colliding with the foot pill. And 460px caps it on desktop, because a menu the size of a dinner plate on a 27-inch display is not more usable, just louder.

8.4 Why percentages, and not pixels

All of this geometry is expressed as percentages of the dial's width, and that is not a stylistic preference — it is a requirement of the mechanism. The sectors are carved with clip-path: polygon(…), and percentage coordinates in a clip-path resolve against the element's own box. So a sector defined in percentages is correct at any dial size, and the single --dial clamp resizes the entire construction — sixteen polygons, five hairlines, one mask — with no recomputation and no JavaScript listening to a resize event.

It also means the figures in this book, which are drawn in the same percentage space and then scaled, are dimensionally identical to what the browser clips. There is no translation step in which an error could hide.

One source of truth for the radii

The module's README states this as a rule for anyone touching the geometry: RINGS defines the bands; the drawn boundary hairlines and the mask that carves the centre hole are both derived from the radii the component actually used and handed to CSS inline. Do not re-type those numbers in radial.css — a hairline sitting where the bands are not is invisible until someone opens the menu.

Chapter Nine

The double deck

Sixteen destinations on one ring gives each 22.5° of arc. At the dial's inner radius on a phone that is a sliver about 20 pixels wide — too narrow to label, too narrow to hit reliably, and too narrow to tell apart from its neighbours. The dial is double-decked because of that number.

9.1 The arithmetic of crowding

A ring divided into n sectors gives each 360/n degrees. The usable arc length of one sector, at radius r, is 2πr/n minus the divider gap. What matters for usability is that arc length at the sector's narrowest point — its inner edge — because that is where the target is thinnest and where two neighbours are closest together.

With sixteen destinations the split is six inner and ten outer, so the inner ring's sectors are 60° wide and the outer ring's are 36° — and crucially, the outer ring's sectors are 36° at a larger radius, where 36° of arc is 90 pixels at the band's inner edge rather than 40. The longer circumference is doing the work.

9.3 The count is not fixed

It is easy to read "sixteen destinations" as a constant. It is not — it is the count that survives filtering, and the filter runs on every render:

Signed out, Inbox and Admin both drop, leaving fourteen. Signed in as a non-admin, fifteen. Signed in as an admin, sixteen. This is the property that makes the geometry defensible rather than merely clever. A hand-laid dial would need three layouts maintained in parallel, and would break the day someone added a seventeenth destination. This one has no layout to maintain: it has a catalog and a function.

9.4 Priority, encoded as radius

The split takes the first six items in catalog order for the inner deck and the rest for the outer. So the catalog's order is not merely a rendering order — it is the priority hierarchy, and it is visible in the geometry: closer to the centre means more important.

The default order puts Home, Explore, Inbox, Creator Studio, Library and Communities on the inner deck, and Store, Arcade, Predictions, Developer, Services and the four RMH Ventures microsites plus Admin on the outer. That is a defensible reading of the platform: the social core inside, the destinations and ventures outside.

It also means users can change it. The platform ships a reorder-and-hide editor for the navigation, validated against the same stable ids, and its saved order is applied through orderNavItems(). A user who lives in the Arcade can promote it to the inner deck, and the dial re-carves. One exception is pinned by construction: Admin's id is deliberately absent from the orderable set, so it always falls to the end of the outer ring and can never be hidden.

The forward-safety property

orderNavItems() puts items present in the saved order first, in that order, then appends anything not mentioned in its default relative position. So a destination shipped after a user saved their preferences still appears, rather than vanishing because it was not in their list. For a dial whose geometry is derived from the resulting array, that property is load-bearing: a silently dropped item would not just be missing, it would silently change the angle of every remaining sector.

Chapter Ten

Carving one destination

One function, thirteen lines, called once per destination per render. It walks out along the outer arc and back along the inner one, sampling ten steps each way, and returns a polygon() string. Everything the dial is — its shape, its hit regions, its dividers — comes out of this.

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|74-94|ringSector()}}

10.1 The walk

The polygon is built in two passes. The first walks the outer radius from a0 to a1 in ten equal steps, emitting eleven points. The second walks the inner radius back from a1 to a0, emitting eleven more. Twenty-two points, closed implicitly by polygon().

Each point is 50 + r·cos(θ), 50 + r·sin(θ) — the centre of the dial is (50%, 50%), and r is a percentage of the width, so the whole thing lands in the coordinate space clip-path expects with no conversion.

{{fig:sectorWalk}}

Figure 10.1 — The walk, on an exaggerated 80° sector. Filled dots are the eleven outer samples; hollow dots are the eleven inner samples on the way back. The ring at the centre of the band is the centroid the glyph is positioned at.

10.2 Why ten steps

steps = 10 is the only tuning number in the function, and it is a straightforward accuracy-versus-size trade. A polygon approximates an arc with chords, and the maximum deviation of a chord from its arc — the sagitta — is r(1 − cos(φ/2)) for a chord subtending φ.

Chord error at the rim (r = 50% of a 460px dial = 230px)
Sector angleφ per stepSagittaVisible?
120° (n=3)12.0°1.26pxmarginal on a hairline edge
90° (n=4)9.0°0.71pxno
60° (n=6, inner deck)6.0°0.32pxno
36° (n=10, outer deck)3.6°0.11pxno
22.5° (n=16)2.25°0.04pxno

At the shipped counts the error is a fraction of a device pixel, well inside antialiasing. Note the pleasant property that the error falls as the dial gets busier: more sectors means narrower sectors means shorter steps. The worst case is the single-ring mode with very few destinations — and even a three-item dial is off by about a pixel at the rim, on an edge that is itself antialiased against a blurred backdrop.

The cost side: each point is about 14 characters of inline style, so 22 points is roughly 320 characters per sector, times two layers, times sixteen destinations — around 10KB of inline clip-path in the DOM. Doubling the steps to 20 would double that for no perceptible gain. Halving it to 5 would put a 60° sector's error at 1.3px, which on the inner deck's crisp mode is visible as faceting.

10.3 The angular gap

Sectors do not touch. Each is inset from its nominal span by half a gap on each side:

const seg = 360 / n;
const gap = Math.min(1.4, seg * 0.06);
const a0 = -90 + i * seg + gap / 2;
const a1 = -90 + (i + 1) * seg - gap / 2;

The gap is 6% of the segment, capped at 1.4°. The proportional term keeps the dividers visually consistent as the count changes — a fixed 1.4° gap between 22.5° sectors is a much heavier line than between 90° ones. The cap stops the gap becoming absurd when there are very few, very wide sectors: at four destinations, 6% of 90° would be a 5.4° gap, a channel wide enough to read as a missing sector rather than a divider.

In practice the cap is what is active at every shipped count, so the dividers are a consistent ~3 pixels. The proportional term is insurance for a future dial with many more destinations, where it degrades gracefully rather than closing the gaps entirely.

10.4 Starting at twelve o'clock

The -90 in the angle expression is the whole of the dial's orientation logic. CSS angles put 0° at three o'clock and increase clockwise; subtracting 90° puts the first sector's leading edge at twelve. Sectors then proceed clockwise, which is the reading order for every left-to-right language and — more importantly — the direction a right thumb sweeps naturally from the bottom-centre of a phone.

An open question about right-to-left locales

The site ships two RTL locales, and the dial's sector order does not mirror in them. Whether it should is genuinely unclear: a dial is not a line of text, and the correspondence between reading direction and rotational direction is a convention rather than a rule — clocks run clockwise in Arabic-speaking countries too. It is listed in Chapter 30 among the things still open, because the honest answer is that nobody has tested it with RTL readers.

10.5 The return value is three things at once

The polygon string produced here is used as a clip-path, and that single fact gives it three jobs simultaneously.

  1. It is the shape. The sector's fill is clipped to it, so the visible sector is exactly this polygon.
  2. It is the hit region. A clip-path bounds hit-testing as well as painting. The anchor element is a full-size square overlaying the whole dial; clipped, it only catches pointer events inside its own sector. No hit-testing code exists in the component at all.
  3. It is the divider. The gaps between adjacent polygons are where the bed shows through, so the dividers are defined by the absence of clip rather than by any drawn line.

Getting all three from one declaration is the reason this component is 521 lines rather than fifteen hundred. It is also the reason the art layer needs a separate, slightly wider polygon, which is Chapter 13.

Chapter Eleven

The hole, the mask and the hairlines

Three pieces of derived geometry, all of which exist so that a change to RINGS cannot leave the dial internally inconsistent. Each is computed from the radii the component actually used and handed to CSS as an inline custom property.

11.1 The bounds array

After carving the sectors, the component collects the distinct band boundaries it used:

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|194-198|The boundary radii, deduplicated and sorted}}

For the double deck that yields [19, 34, 36, 50]; for single-ring mode, [19, 50]. Two consumers read it: the hairline elements, one per boundary, and the centre-hole mask, which takes bounds[0].

The point of routing both through this array rather than re-typing the numbers is stated as a rule in the module README, and it is the kind of rule that only gets written after somebody has been bitten: a hairline drawn at a radius where no band boundary exists is invisible until someone opens the menu, and then it is a mystery line.

11.2 The hole

The dial's divider bed is a full disc, so without intervention the middle of the dial is a solid grey circle behind the orb — a bullseye. The hole is cut with a mask:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|1019-1031|Carving the centre out of the bed}}

And the radius comes from the component:

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|334-343|The hole radius, derived}}

There are two unit conversions in that one expression, and both are easy to get wrong.

Radius versus width. The band radii are fractions of the dial's width. A radial-gradient stop with closest-side on a square element is a fraction of the gradient's radius, which is half the width. So the value has to be doubled: 19 becomes 38%.

The border straddle. The innermost hairline is drawn on radius 19, centred on it, so half its 2px width falls inside the hole. If the mask cut exactly at 19, that half would be masked away and the innermost line would render at half weight — subtly lighter than the other three, for no visible reason. So the mask is pulled inward by the border width: calc(38% - var(--dial-level-w)).

Both of these are the kind of detail that is invisible when right and inexplicable when wrong, which is precisely why the arithmetic lives in one place with a comment rather than being distributed as magic numbers.

11.3 The hairlines

The boundaries between decks are drawn, not implied. Four elements — one per entry in bounds — each a circle with a border:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|1118-1149|One hairline per band boundary}}

Two decisions in there are worth pulling out.

Real bordered elements, not gradient stops. The obvious alternative is hard colour stops in the bed's own radial-gradient. It is cheaper and it antialiases badly: a hard stop in a gradient is resolved at whatever resolution the gradient is rasterised at, and at some dial sizes it produces a visibly stepped ring. A bordered element is antialiased by the same path that draws every other rounded box on the page, at any size.

Width, again, not radius. --r is a percentage of the dial's width, and the element needs a diameter, so width: calc(var(--r) * 2 + var(--dial-level-w)). The extra border width is the same straddle correction as the mask, from the other side: it centres the line on the boundary rather than placing it just inside.

11.4 Why a drawn edge rather than a wider gap

The gap between the decks is only 2 units — about 9 pixels at the largest dial — and an earlier instinct was to widen it instead of drawing a line. The reason that is wrong is stated compactly in the CSS:

They are what separates the decks: the gap alone reads as a missing fill, a drawn edge reads as structure. components/radial/radial.css — the level borders

This is a real perceptual difference and not a matter of taste. An unfilled channel between two rings of sectors is ambiguous — it could be a boundary, or it could be a row of sectors that failed to render. A line is unambiguous: something drew that, on purpose. The dial has two decks, and the line is the sentence that says so.

It matters more once the decks start moving. During the open animation the two rings counter-rotate into alignment, which means mid-animation the sectors are not where the structure says they should be. The hairlines are the only thing holding the dial's shape together at that moment, which is why they fade in on the same curve as the wedges rather than appearing at the end.

Chapter Twelve

Labels, centroids and budgets

"Adaptive Intelligence" is nineteen characters. It has to fit in a sector that is 36° wide and 64 pixels deep, without escaping a clip-path that will silently cut it in half. This chapter is about the single most fiddly geometry in the dial, and about a bug — AUD-504(c) — that is a small lesson in which direction a text box grows.

12.1 The centroid

Each destination's glyph and label are positioned at the centroid of its visible band — the midpoint of its angular span, at the mid-radius of its ring:

const rm = (band.r0 + band.r1) / 2;
const am = ((a0 + a1) / 2) * DEG;
cx: 50 + rm * Math.cos(am),
cy: 50 + rm * Math.sin(am),

These are handed to the label wrapper as inline left and top percentages, and the wrapper is pulled back by half its own size with transform: translate(-50%, -50%). So the label box is centred on the centroid, which is the visual centre of the sector.

As noted in Chapter 8, the annulus form makes this stable: rm does not depend on the sector's angle, so all six inner glyphs sit on one circle at radius 26.5 and all ten outer glyphs on another at radius 43, no matter how the counts change.

12.2 The direction the box grows

Here is the bug. A label box centred on a centroid grows in two directions, and they are not equivalent.

Width grows angularly. The box is axis-aligned, but the sector is a wedge. Growing the box horizontally pushes its corners across the sector's radial edges — across the clip-path — and the clip cuts them off. Silently. There is no overflow indicator, no ellipsis; the text simply stops at the sector boundary.

Height grows radially. Growing the box vertically pushes it into the band's depth, where the inner deck has 69 pixels and the outer 64. There is room there.

The first implementation set a generous max-width and let long names take one line. The audit's finding is the predictable result: "Adaptive Intelligence" clips at its wedge edge, "Predictions" overflows the disc. Two different failures from the same cause — one name was cut by the clip, the other was long enough to reach past the rim before the clip caught it.

The label itself is clamped to three lines with overflow-wrap: anywhere and hyphens: auto, so a very long single word — a hypothetical destination named something unhelpful — degrades to a hyphenated wrap rather than escaping its box. That clamp is a hard bound: a destination name can never spill across its neighbours, whatever it is, in whatever locale.

AUD-504(c), and the one-line clamp that made it worse

An intermediate fix clamped labels to a single line, which stopped the clipping and introduced a new failure: "Communities" became "Communi…". The comment recording the correction is precise about why two lines are affordable — "the inner band is deep enough for two lines (icon + label ≈ 50px in a ~69px band at the largest dial), so the shorter arc costs width, not lines." Depth was never the scarce resource. Width was.

12.4 Icon-only below 480px

Under 480 pixels the labels are not rendered at all. The dial at 320px is 275 pixels across; the outer band is 38 pixels deep and its sectors are 36° wide, which at that radius is about 37 pixels of arc. There is no typography that puts "Adaptive Intelligence" in a 37×38 box.

So the phone dial is sixteen glyphs. The audit's judgement on that was blunt and correct — sixteen unlabelled glyphs as the site's sole navigation is recall, not recognition — and the fix is the centre caption in Chapter 24, which puts the name of the focused destination in the one place on a 320px dial that has room for a word: the hole in the middle.

The breakpoint itself is 480px, registered as the platform's xs breakpoint. Above it, labels appear and the caption is hidden, because each wedge then carries its own name and a caption would be a duplicate. The two mechanisms are exact complements: exactly one of them is showing a label at any width.

12.5 Where the glyph sits inside the box

The glyph and label are a two-row grid with a 0.3rem gap, icon above label, both centred. The icon is 1.3rem square; the label is 0.6875rem at weight 600 with 1.1 line-height. Total for icon plus two lines of label: about 50 pixels, in a 64-to-69-pixel band. That is the arithmetic quoted in the comment above, and it is the reason the label budget can afford two lines rather than one.

One detail that matters for input: the whole inner wrapper is pointer-events: none. Clicks fall through the glyph to the clipped anchor underneath. Without that, the label box — an axis-aligned rectangle overlapping several sectors' territory near the dividers — would steal clicks for its own sector from its neighbours, in a region where the user is aiming at a visible boundary. Chapter 22 covers the rest of the hit-testing chain.

Chapter Thirteen

The art bleed

One constant, ART_BLEED_DEG = 0.25, and a comment four times its length. It exists because the goo filter eats a couple of pixels off every edge it touches, and because the correct compensation is angular but not radial.

13.1 What the filter does to an edge

The goo filter blurs its input and then runs the blurred alpha through a steep contrast ramp, which snaps it back to a hard edge. Chapter 19 covers the mechanism; what matters here is a side effect. The ramp's threshold sits at roughly the halfway point of the blurred alpha gradient, so the recovered edge is not where the original edge was — it is pulled inward, by an amount that depends on the blur radius and on how much of the neighbourhood was filled.

For a large shape, that inward pull is a couple of pixels and nobody notices. For the 3-pixel channel between two sectors, both sides pull inward by a couple of pixels and the channel becomes 7 or 8 — which is no longer a hairline divider but a visible gutter. The dial's careful proportional gap arithmetic from Chapter 10 is undone by the filter that was supposed to make it look liquid.

13.2 The compensation

So the decorative layer draws each sector slightly wider than the hit layer, and only in the angular direction:

13.3 Why not radially

The interesting half of the decision is what the bleed deliberately does not do. It is applied to the angles only; the radii passed to ringSector() for the art layer are the band's own, unmodified.

Radially, the erosion is welcome. The boundaries between decks are meant to be pronounced — that is the entire argument of Chapter 11 — and a filter that pulls the sectors slightly back from those boundaries makes the drawn hairlines sit in a clean channel rather than being crowded by fill on both sides. Compensating there would fight the structure the hairlines are drawing.

So the same filter artefact is a defect in one axis and a feature in the other, and the constant is named and commented so that the asymmetry looks deliberate rather than forgotten.

The invariant the bleed must not break

Hit testing always uses the exact geometry. The bled polygon is only ever applied to the aria-hidden art layer, which is pointer-events: none; the anchors use w.clip, never w.clipArt. So the bleed cannot steal a neighbour's clicks, and a user pressing a pixel that looks like it belongs to one sector but geometrically belongs to another gets the geometric answer. That is a 1-pixel discrepancy at the dividers, in exchange for never having a target whose visible extent and clickable extent disagree by more than that.

13.4 What this chapter is really about

A quarter of a degree is a preposterous thing to have a constant for. It is in this book because it is a compact example of what building the dial actually consisted of: not the radial idea, which took an afternoon, but a long tail of interactions between a geometric construction and the rendering pipeline it is drawn by.

The dial's shape is defined by clip paths. Clip paths bound hit-testing. Hit-testing has to be exact. Filters move edges. Filters must not touch text. Text inherits colour from the element it is inside, which must therefore be the sector. Blend modes composite against a stacking context, not against what you see. Masks measure radius where the geometry measures width. Every one of those is a constraint imposed by the platform rather than by the design, and the design's job was to survive all of them without becoming a special case.

The radial idea took an afternoon. The quarter of a degree took a week.
