Chapter Twenty-nine

How it is checked

Ten CI workflows, a consistency test that fails the build on hand-rolled tab strips, a nine-point definition of done, and a manual floor of three themes by two widths. None of it would have caught most of Part VII.

Read that table honestly and the conclusion is uncomfortable: not one of these checks could have caught a single defect in Chapter 28. White-on-white glyphs are valid CSS. A tint of a tint type-checks. A black plate uncovered by a rotation compiles. A mobile-only media query around the floating-stack coordination is well-formed. A label box clipped by a wedge's clip-path is not a lint error.

Everything in Part VII was found by rendering the page and looking at it.

29.2 The manual floor

Which is why the platform's definition of done ends with a rendering requirement rather than a test. Its ninth point: the change was actually looked at in default, .style-light and .style-high-contrast, at a phone width and a desktop width, and once with reduced motion on. Three themes by two widths is stated as the floor, with the audit's thirteen-viewport matrix as the extended version.

For this component specifically, the module README adds two of its own: verify the hub bloom and the wheel on a 320-pixel-wide screen when changing geometry, and check that nothing leaves the viewport.

29.3 The audit as an instrument

The July audit is the closest thing the project has to a systematic check, and its design is worth describing because it is reusable.

Forty-plus routes at thirteen viewports in three themes, plus reduced motion, plus first-run overlays, plus scripted interactions — the hub's bloom among them, captured deliberately rather than incidentally. Every finding names the route, the viewport, the evidence capture, the suspected source file with line numbers, the design principle violated, and a fix direction. Findings carry stable identifiers, and a remediation order that groups them by root cause.

Two properties make it more than a screenshot dump. It is addressed to agents as well as humans, which forces every finding to be independently actionable — no "the spacing looks off here". And it records its own harness incidents: overlays that blocked captures, seven captures that silently rendered signed-out, and the hub captures that skipped because of the accessible-name drift in Chapter 23. An audit that does not report its own failures is an audit whose gaps are invisible.

The gap this leaves

An audit is a snapshot. It ran once, on 28 July, and two commits later the findings it produced were substantially fixed — at which point the instrument was spent. Nothing in CI re-renders the hub at thirteen viewports on every pull request. So the class of defect that dominates Part VII is, today, caught by a process that runs when somebody decides to run it. That is the single most consequential open item in this record, and it is not on the list in Chapter 30 because it is not about this control.

29.4 What a real regression suite would need to assert

Offered as a specification, since the analysis is already done. A useful automated check on this component would assert, per theme and per viewport:

  1. Every wedge's computed color meets 4.5:1 against its own computed background, in resting, hover, focus and active states.
  2. The dial's bed and sector fills differ by a measurable amount, so the dividers are visible.
  3. No wedge label's rendered box exceeds its sector's clip bounds.
  4. Every wedge's hit region is at least 44 pixels in its smaller dimension — which would currently fail at 320px, correctly.
  5. The orb's rect does not intersect the rect of any other floating control, or of any element the page marks as content.
  6. Nothing in the shell overflows the viewport horizontally at 320px.
  7. With reduced motion, the dial's computed transform at rest equals its computed transform when open.
  8. The orb's accessible name equals the shipped catalog string, not the defaultValue.

Eight assertions, all mechanically checkable in a headless browser, and between them they cover five of the six patterns in Chapter 28. The sixth — decoration in the same pixels as information — needs a human.

Chapter Thirty

What is still open

Seven things, in rough order of how much they matter. The first is the one that could invalidate the whole design, and it is the one nobody has solved.

30.1 Discovery — no first-run hint

Below 1120 pixels, the entire navigation of a platform with sixteen destinations is one unlabelled circular mark. AUD-118 records it; nothing has shipped.

This is the item that could make the rest of this document moot. Every argument in Part I — thumb reach, collapsed controls, priority encoded as radius — presupposes that the user opens the dial. A user who does not recognise the orb as a menu has a worse experience than they would have had with a hamburger, because at least a hamburger is a learned convention.

Why it is hard: too quiet and the hint is decoration; too loud and it is an interstitial, on a platform that already has enough first-run overlays that the audit had to suppress several to capture anything. The audit's own suggestion — a transient "Menu" caption below the orb on first visit — is probably right, and the open questions are how long it persists, what dismisses it, and whether it re-appears for a signed-out visitor who has cleared storage.

30.2 Keyboard semantics

The dial is role="menu" with menuitem children and no arrow-key handling, no focus trap, and no focus restore on close. Chapter 23 states each gap. The honest position is that it currently behaves like a labelled list of links, which is usable and is not what its own ARIA role promises.

The design question underneath is real: on a two-deck ring, what should Up and Down do? Move between decks is the obvious answer, and Left/Right rotating within a deck is the other half, but that means Right at the last sector wraps to the first — and a wrapping tab order inside a document that does not wrap is its own confusion.

30.3 Touch targets at 320 pixels

Wedges are 41 pixels deep on the inner deck and 38 on the outer at the smallest supported width, against a 44-pixel floor. Chapter 22 gives the mitigating argument — the arc dimension is 55 to 76 pixels, and a miss lands in a drawn gap rather than on a neighbour — and it is a mitigating argument, not compliance.

The obvious fix is to raise the dial's 86vw below 360 pixels. The unresolved part is the foot pill: at 320×568 the dial and the pill already have little room between them, and growing the dial pushes them together. Nobody has worked through whether the pill should shrink, move, or overlap the dial's rim at that width.

30.4 Landscape phones

AUD-105: at 844×390, the top bar, cookie bar and orb between them left roughly 150 pixels of usable content height. The audit's note is that this needs a design decision rather than a fix — collapse the orb to a corner under a max-height condition, or shrink the chrome, or accept it.

The dial itself is fine in landscape; 70vh binds and it renders at 273 pixels. It is the docked state that is the problem, and specifically the interaction of three independently-reasonable pieces of fixed chrome on a viewport 390 pixels tall.

30.5 Right-to-left locales

Sector order does not mirror in the two RTL locales, and nobody has tested whether it should. Chapter 10's note stands: the correspondence between reading direction and rotational direction is a convention, not a rule, and clocks run clockwise everywhere. But "we did not think about it" and "we decided it was right" are different states, and this is the former.

30.6 The parked lens optic

The platform's SVG displacement lens — the effect that bends the backdrop through a height field at a pane's edge — is parked, because current Chromium composites the displacement map into the bevel rather than bending the backdrop through it. The filters and the supporting module still ship; re-enabling is restoring two @supports upgrades and one initialisation call.

The hub does not use it and would not obviously benefit. It is listed because anyone reading the design language will find .glass-refract documented as live and should know that one optic in it is not.

30.7 A continuous visual check

Covered in Chapter 29 and repeated here because it is the item most likely to determine whether the next twenty commits produce another Part VII. The audit was an instrument that ran once. The defects it found were the kind that only a rendered page reveals. There is no per-pull-request equivalent.

30.8 What is not on this list

Three things that might be expected and are deliberately absent.

Performance. Nothing in the hub is a measured performance problem. The expensive things are gated, the animation is compositor-only, and the one full-viewport backdrop-filter exists for a few seconds when the user asks for it.

The two-tap cost of going home. Chapter 5 defends it, and the defence stands: one unambiguous behaviour on the site's only universal control is worth more than one saved tap on a destination the user is usually already at.

The radial shape itself. This is the thing a reader might most expect to be hedged, and it is not. The geometry earns its place: it collapses three controls into one, it encodes priority as radius, it costs no layout space, it derives itself from a catalog, and it puts every target inside a thumb's sweep. If the design fails, it will fail on §30.1 — because people did not know it was there — and not because a dial was the wrong shape for what it does.

The open questions are about discovery, keyboards and small screens. None of them is about the circle.

Appendix E

Glossary

Annulus sector
A ring segment: the region between two radii over a span of angles. The dial's target shape, chosen over the pie slice for the four reasons in Chapter 8.
Art layer
The decorative, goo-filtered, aria-hidden copy of the sector geometry. Carries the fills and nothing readable.
Bed
The dial's own background, visible through the angular gaps between sectors, which is what the dividers are made of.
Bleed
The angular widening applied to the art layer to pay back the edge erosion caused by the goo filter's alpha threshold. ART_BLEED_DEG = 0.25 of the gap, each side.
Bloom
The open animation, collectively. Also, separately, the mark's one-shot mount animation.
Deck
One of the dial's two concentric rings. Inner deck = the first six catalog entries.
Foot pill
The floating capsule at the bottom of the open overlay: identity, settings, sign-out, close — or a sign-in call to action when signed out.
Goo
The metaball effect: blur, then a steep ramp on alpha, then the sharp original blended back. Three radii, in LiquidGoo.
Hit layer
The real, unfiltered links — one per destination, each clipped to its exact sector, each containing its own icon and label.
Hole
The masked-out centre of the dial, radius 19, which the orb glides into. Also where the centre caption lives below 480px.
Hub
The whole control: orb, aura, scrim, overlay, blur, dial and foot pill.
Level hairline
One drawn circle per band boundary, so the decks are separated by an edge rather than by an absence of fill.
Liquid Glass / Radial Avant-Garde Glass
The platform's material and, with the radial architecture, its design language.
Orb
The fixed, bottom-centre disc carrying the RMH mark. The trigger, and the centre the dial radiates from.
Phase
closed | open | closing. One attribute on the wrapper; the entire animation state.
Ring / band
Used interchangeably for a deck's radial extent. RINGS in the source; "deck" in this document where the distinction from a single ring matters.
Token
A --site-* CSS custom property that every theme redefines. The contract the hub is built out of.
Wedge
One destination's sector, in either layer. Sixteen of them, signed in as an admin.
