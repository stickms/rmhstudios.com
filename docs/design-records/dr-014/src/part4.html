Chapter Fourteen

One synchronous motion

The orb glides, the blur expands, the dial scales and fades, two rings of sectors counter-rotate, four hairlines appear, and a capsule rises from the bottom edge. All of it in half a second, all of it on one clock, none of it orchestrated by anything but shared duration constants.

14.1 Why 500ms

Half a second is slow for a menu. The conventional range for an overlay is 150–250ms, and anything past 300ms is usually a sign that someone fell in love with an easing curve.

The hub is long deliberately, because the motion is doing work that a fade cannot. The orb has to travel roughly 40% of the viewport's height, and the user has to be able to follow it — the entire identity claim of Chapter 5, that the menu grows out of the thing you touched, only lands if the eye can track the thing as it moves. Travel that distance in 200ms and the orb does not glide, it teleports, and the connection between the button and the dial becomes an inference again.

The easing does most of the perceptual work: cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1), the platform's --ease-glass. It is aggressively front-loaded — roughly 80% of the distance is covered in the first 40% of the duration — so the motion feels fast while lasting long enough to be followed. The audit's own note confirms the subjective result: the bloom reads as "fully formed by about 120ms", despite the declared duration being four times that. The tail is settling, not waiting.

14.3 The stagger, and what it is applied to

Each wedge wrapper carries its bloom order as a custom property, and the open state turns that into a delay:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|1099-1116|The stagger, on opacity only}}

Two details are load-bearing.

The stagger is on opacity, not on transform. Sixteen wedges × 20ms is a 320ms spread; applied to the rotation, that would mean each sector arriving at its resting angle at a different moment, and a ring would appear to unwind rather than turn. Applied to opacity, the ring turns as one body while its members fade up in sequence. The comment in the source says exactly this: "the spin is deliberately NOT staggered: every wedge of a ring shares one delay, so the ring turns as a body."

The stagger only applies on open. The delay lives in the [data-phase='open'] block, so on close it reverts to 0ms and all sixteen fade together as the blur contracts. Closing a menu should be one gesture, not a reverse choreography — the user has already decided; the interface should get out of the way at once.

The order itself runs inner deck first, then outer, in catalog order, because order is assigned as the groups are walked. So the bloom radiates outward, which is the same direction the whole design says things move in.

14.4 What is not animated

Three absences are worth noting, because each was a decision.

No motion on the glyphs. The icons and labels do not scale, rotate or slide; they inherit their sector's opacity and nothing else. Animating text inside a rotating element means animating text through sub-pixel positions, which on most rendering paths means animating text through several different antialiasing decisions — the visible symptom is shimmering type. The glyphs simply fade with their sector.

No motion on the hairlines' geometry. They fade, they do not grow. A hairline circle scaling up would sweep its 2px stroke across a range of radii, and a stroked circle at non-integer scale is where antialiasing artefacts live. They are declared with a transform transition — so the property is there if it is ever needed — but the open state changes only opacity.

No colour transitions during the bloom. Sector fills are static during open and close; the only colour transitions in the hub are the 180ms hover and focus responses. Animating a color-mix() of two tokens is not something to do sixteen times per frame for aesthetic reasons.

The motion contract, stated once

Transform and opacity only. Every animated property in the hub is one of those two, plus clip-path on the blur — all compositor-friendly, none of them triggering layout. There is no animated width, height, top, left, margin, padding, border-radius or filter anywhere in the module. That is a platform-wide rule, and it is what makes a nine-layer synchronous animation viable on a phone.

14.5 Why there is no animation library here

The rest of the site uses framer-motion freely, and the hub deliberately does not. Its docblock says so in one line: "framer-motion-free (CSS transitions/clip-path) to stay light on mobile."

The reasoning is a bundle question and a correctness question. The bundle question is obvious: this component ships on every page of the site, including to phones, and its animation needs are fully expressible in CSS. The correctness question is subtler and more interesting — a JavaScript animation runs on the main thread's clock, which means a busy main thread produces a stuttering menu, and the main thread on a page that has just started rendering a feed is busy. CSS transitions on transform and opacity run on the compositor. The hub opens smoothly while the page behind it is still hydrating, which is exactly the moment a user is most likely to open it.

The cost is that everything has to be expressible as a declarative state, which is a real constraint — Chapter 16 is a bug that exists precisely because of it. That is a fair price for a menu that does not stutter on a mid-range Android during hydration.

Chapter Fifteen

The phase machine

Three states, one string, one timer. The interesting one is closing, which exists for a reason that catches out a great many overlay components: a component that unmounts its overlay on close cannot have a close animation.

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|14-18|The phase type}}

15.1 The problem closing solves

The naive shape for a menu is a boolean. open ? <Overlay/> : null, or a class that is toggled. Both have the same defect: the moment the state flips to closed, the overlay is gone — either unmounted from the tree or set to visibility: hidden — and a transition on an element that is no longer rendered does not run. The menu vanishes instead of closing.

The usual workarounds are an animation library that manages exit transitions, or a transitionend listener, or an animation-fill-mode trick. The hub takes the simplest available option: a third state that means "still visible, animating out", and a timer that leaves it.

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|205-220|close() and toggle()}}

Both handlers clear the pending timer first. That is what makes the machine interruptible: tap the orb three times quickly and you do not accumulate three timers racing to set the phase, you get one timer, restarted, and the CSS transitions reverse from wherever they currently are.

15.2 The CSS side of the same idea

The overlay's visibility is keyed to both live phases, not just open:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|931-944|The overlay is mounted for open AND closing}}

And every animated layer has the same shape: a resting declaration, and an override under [data-phase='open']. The closing phase deliberately has almost no rules of its own — it inherits the resting state, which is precisely how the reversal happens. Two exceptions exist, both about stacking: the orb and the aura keep their raised z-index during closing, so the orb stays above the overlay for the whole return journey rather than popping behind the frost halfway home.

15.3 The timer's reduced-motion case

Both the close paths use reduced ? 0 : MOTION_MS. Under reduced motion there are no transitions to wait for, so waiting half a second before unmounting would leave an invisible, non-interactive overlay covering the screen — technically pointer-events: auto, technically transparent, and completely indefensible.

This is one of only two places in the component where a preference is read in JavaScript rather than in CSS, and it is the exception that proves the rule: the platform's guidance is that degradation belongs in the token layer, and the reason this one cannot is that a setTimeout is not a CSS property. A media query cannot shorten a timer.

15.4 Escape, wheel, and the effects that own them

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|234-249|Keyboard and wheel, while the menu is live}}

Three things about that effect.

It is keyed on phase and returns early when closed, so no global listener exists while the menu is shut — a hub on every page of the site must not leave a keydown handler on the window at all times.

The wheel listener is { passive: false }, because it calls preventDefault(), and a passive listener may not. It is attached to the overlay rather than the window so it cannot suppress scrolling anywhere else, and it checks e.cancelable first, because a wheel event during an already-started scroll may not be cancellable and calling preventDefault() on it is a console warning for nothing.

It exists at all because touch-action: none — the technique that blocks touch panning, Chapter 25 — does not cover pointer devices. Two mechanisms, two input classes, one outcome.

15.5 Focus, on the next frame

{{code:components/radial/RadialHub.tsx|251-257|Moving focus into the bloomed dial}}

The requestAnimationFrame is not decoration. On the render that sets the phase to open, the dial's links have just had their tabIndex changed from -1 to 0 and the overlay's visibility has just changed; focusing synchronously in that same commit can land on an element the browser still considers unfocusable. Deferring by one frame puts the focus call after style resolution.

The query — a[href], [tabindex] — takes the first focusable thing in the dial, which is the first wedge, which is the first catalog entry, which is Home. So a keyboard user's focus lands on the most likely destination without any explicit initial-focus configuration. The cleanup cancels the frame, so a menu opened and closed within a single frame does not steal focus after the fact.

What this focus handling does not do

There is no focus trap. Tab from the last wedge and focus leaves the dial for the foot pill, and beyond that for the document. There is also no explicit focus restore to the orb on close. Both are noted in Chapter 30 as gaps: a modal navigation overlay conventionally traps focus and returns it, and this one does neither. What it does have is tabIndex={-1} on every control while closed, so the closed hub is genuinely absent from the tab order rather than being a set of invisible tab stops — which is the more common and more serious version of this bug.

Chapter Sixteen

Counter-rotation, and the bed that flashed black

The two decks arrive by spinning into alignment from opposite directions — inner anticlockwise, outer clockwise. It is the dial's signature gesture, it cost one line of CSS, and it broke the dial the day it landed, in a way that is a small classic of front-end debugging.

16.1 The gesture

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|1106-1116|Two decks, two directions}}

That is the entire effect. Each wedge wrapper is inset: 0 over the whole dial square, so its transform origin is already the dial's centre and a rotation carries its clipped sector around that centre — no transform-origin declaration needed. The resting state is rotated by ±55°; the open state is zero. Every wedge in a ring shares its ring's angle, so the ring turns as a body.

{{fig:spinFrames}}

Figure 16.1 — Five frames of the open, from the resting ±55° to alignment. The decks pass through each other's angular territory on the way, which is what makes the two levels read as independent rings rather than as one disc with a line through it — and which is also what uncovers the plate underneath them.

The reason for opposite directions is legibility of structure. Two rings rotating the same way read as one object turning. Two rings rotating against each other cannot be anything but two objects, and the viewer learns the dial's most important structural fact — that it has two decks — from the motion, before any of the labels have finished fading in.

Fifty-five degrees is a little more than one inner sector (60°) and a little more than one and a half outer ones (36°). That is deliberate: the rotation is large enough that every sector visibly passes through a neighbour's resting position, so the eye cannot mistake the motion for a settling wobble.

16.2 The bug

Commit d308f26 added the spin and the hairlines together. It also, without touching it, broke the dial's background.

The dial's divider bed had been --site-border-bright — which in the default theme is pure black. That was a perfectly reasonable choice for as long as the sectors only scaled: at every frame of a scale animation the sectors cover essentially the whole annulus, so the bed is visible only in the 3-pixel channels between them, where a strong dark line is exactly what is wanted.

The moment the sectors rotate, that stops being true. A ring rotated by 55° relative to its resting position does not cover the same annulus — it covers a rotated copy of it, and because the sectors are clipped to a fixed dial box, rotating the wrapper sweeps each sector's polygon around while the dial's own circular boundary stays put. Large regions of the bed are uncovered mid-animation. Uncovered black bed, on a white page, for a few hundred milliseconds, sixteen times over: the whole dial flashed black on open.

The shape of the failure

Nothing in the diff touched the background. The background's value had been correct for the entire life of the component. What changed was the coverage of the layer above it — and a value whose correctness depends on always being covered is a value with an undeclared dependency on the animation. This is a general hazard with "it never shows anyway" reasoning: it is true until a motion change makes it false, and then it is a full-screen flash rather than a subtle regression.

16.3 The fix, and why it is not just a colour change

The bed became a light plate — color-mix(in srgb, var(--site-surface-opaque) 86%, var(--site-text) 14%) — a shade darker than the sectors that sit on it rather than an emphatic dark line. The comment recording it is one of the more useful in the codebase:

{{code:components/radial/radial.css|989-999|The bed, and why it is light}}

The important clause is the last one: "the level borders below draw the structure that the black bed used to imply, so the plate only has to be a shade darker than the sectors that sit on it."

The two halves of commit d308f26 are not independent. The spin uncovered the bed, which forced the bed to become light, which removed the strong dark structure the black bed had been providing, which is why the same commit had to add the drawn hairlines. Adding the spin without the hairlines would have produced a dial that no longer flashed but also no longer read as two decks. The commit's title — "spin the double-decked dial open, and draw the borders between levels" — is two changes because it had to be.

A value that is only correct while something covers it is not a value. It is a bet on the animation.

16.4 The uncovered-area arithmetic

It is worth quantifying, because the number explains why this was a flash rather than a flicker.

Each ring's sectors tile their annulus with 1.4° gaps, so at rest they cover about 97% of it. Rotating the ring by 55° does not reduce that coverage — the sectors still tile the annulus, just rotated. What is uncovered is not part of the annulus at all: it is the region where the rotated sector has swung outside the dial's circular clip and been cut off, leaving its former territory empty.

For the outer ring, whose sectors reach the rim, that loss is small. For the inner ring, at radius 19–34, the rotation carries sectors around a circle entirely inside the dial, so nothing is cut and nothing is uncovered — the inner deck simply turns.

What actually produced the flash was the combination with scale. The wrappers rest at scale(0.9) rotate(±55deg). At 0.9 scale, every sector's outer radius is 45 rather than 50, and its inner radius is 17.1 rather than 19 — so the annulus the sectors occupy is smaller than the annulus the bed fills, in both directions, at every frame of the animation. A 5%-wide black band at the rim and a black ring at the hole boundary, growing thinner as the dial scales up. On a white page, that is a flash.

16.5 What the episode is worth

Three transferable observations.

  1. Scale-from-below plus a full-size backdrop is a flash waiting to happen. If a layer rests at scale(<1) and something behind it is full size, the difference is visible for the whole animation. Either scale the backdrop with it, or make the backdrop something that is safe to see.
  2. Adding motion to a static layer changes the requirements of every layer beneath it. The bug was not in the diff; it was in the interaction between the diff and an assumption made elsewhere.
  3. "Only visible in the gaps" should be written down. Had the bed's original declaration carried a comment saying this is only ever seen in the 3px dividers, the person adding the spin would have had a chance to notice the assumption breaking. It now carries a comment several times longer than the declaration.

Chapter Seventeen

The blur, and the degradation ladder

A circle clipped from radius zero to 150vmax, and a set of media queries that decide how much of the hub's character survives on a phone, on a low-memory device, or for a user who has asked the operating system for less motion.

17.1 Why 150vmax

The blur's clip circle has to cover the viewport from a centre point that is the middle of the screen. Half the diagonal of the viewport would suffice — sqrt(w² + h²) / 2 — but CSS has no diagonal unit, and 50vmax is only enough for a square viewport. 150vmax is a comfortable over-provision: three times the larger dimension, which covers any aspect ratio anyone will ever hand it, including a 21:9 ultrawide.

Over-provisioning a clip radius costs nothing. The clipped element is position: absolute; inset: 0, so its box is the viewport regardless; the clip only decides how much of that box paints. A radius larger than the box is simply a fully-painted box. What the animation is actually interpolating is a number that stops mattering once it exceeds the corner distance — which means the visible expansion finishes somewhat before the transition does, and the last portion of the curve is spent invisibly. Given how front-loaded the easing is, that is a feature: the frost reaches the corners early and the tail of the transition is silent.

17.2 What the blur costs

backdrop-filter: blur(20px) saturate(118%) over a full viewport is the single most expensive thing the hub does. It requires the compositor to read back the content behind the element, blur it, and composite the result — every frame, while the clip radius is changing.

It is affordable for exactly one reason: it is transient and modal. It exists only while the menu is open, which is a few seconds at most, during which nothing else on the page is animating and the user is not scrolling — the overlay has blocked that. Compare this with the platform's standing budget for the material, which is at most eight backdrop-filter elements per viewport and zero on repeated list items. The hub spends one, briefly, when the user asks for it.

The tint is a deliberate whisper: color-mix(in srgb, var(--site-bg) 20%, transparent). Twenty percent of the page background, mixed with transparency. Enough to lift contrast for the sectors and glyphs sitting on it; not enough to read as a coloured scrim. The distinction the CSS comment draws is exact — "only a whisper of tint, so it reads as BLUR, not a coloured circle."

17.3 The degradation ladder

The hub's decorative layers are gated, and it is worth being precise about which gate each one sits behind, because the gates are not the same and the difference reflects a judgement about what kind of thing each gate is measuring.

The pattern: cost is gated by width, comfort is gated by preference, and space is gated by width for entirely different reasons. Conflating those is how components end up with a phone that has no labels because someone reasoned about GPU budget when the actual constraint was arc length.

17.4 What reduced motion actually looks like

Worth spelling out, because "respects reduced motion" is often a claim rather than a description.

Under prefers-reduced-motion: reduce, tapping the orb produces: the overlay becoming visible immediately; the blur at full radius immediately; the dial at full opacity and scale immediately; all sixteen sectors at their resting angles, unrotated, fully opaque; the hairlines drawn; the foot pill in place; the orb at the centre. No aura. No goo — the sectors are crisp-edged, which is arguably a clearer dial than the filtered one. And closing sets the phase to closing and then to closed on a 0ms timer, so the overlay is gone within a frame.

The important property is that it is the same dial. Not a fallback list, not a different component, not a simplified layout. Every target is in the same place, at the same size, with the same label. A user who has asked for less motion has asked for less motion, not for a different information architecture.

The one thing reduced motion changes about the layout

Nothing. Deliberately. The wedges' resting transform includes scale(0.9), and it is the transition that is dropped, not the transform — so the open state's scale(1) rotate(0deg) still applies, instantly. If the reduced-motion block had dropped the transforms rather than the transitions, the dial would have rendered at 90% with its decks rotated, permanently. That is a real trap, and the distinction between "remove the animation" and "remove the animated property" is the whole of it.
